Red Horizon is a [fictional, AI-assisted Mars exploration programme](/#mission). Its
[first public step](/#updates) set the science question: how water changed an ancient
equatorial landscape in the Margaritifer Terra region. Its second step is the one that decides
what evidence the mission can actually collect — the kinds of measurement that go on the rover.

The programme has now agreed a payload scope for Red Horizon One: panoramic and navigation
imaging, weather measurement, mineral and rock sensing, and a contact tool for close inspection.
That scope is not a finished instrument list. Which instruments will do those four jobs, and what
they will be called, is still open, and the programme is treating it that way.

## Why several instruments instead of one

Each instrument answers a different kind of question, and a geology result is stronger when the
answers can be checked against each other. A panorama shows where a rock sits in a layered
sequence. A composition measurement says what that rock is made of. A weather record says what
the local environment has been doing to the surface over a season. A close-up inspection shows
the texture and the weathered surface that the wider view cannot resolve.

No single one of those is decisive on its own. Taken together they are the kind of evidence that
can support a statement about whether an area once offered water, chemistry, energy, and
environmental stability — the question this mission exists to work on.

## Looking at the landscape

Panoramic and navigation imaging is the baseline of the payload. A panorama puts a target in
context: what lies around it, how the ground changes from one point to the next, and where a
sampling target sits in the layers above and below it. Navigation cameras do a different job —
they give the rover the stereo views it uses to plan a drive and judge the terrain ahead of it.

Neither is an unusual capability. ESA's Rosalind Franklin rover carries
[PanCam](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Exploration/ExoMars/All_instruments_onboard_Rosalind_Franklin_rover),
which combines wide-angle stereo cameras with a high-resolution camera and filter wheels. NASA's
Perseverance rover carries
[MastCam-Z](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/science-instruments/), a pair
of zoomable cameras on its mast.

## Reading what rocks are made of

Mineral and rock sensing covers the composition question. The standard technique is spectroscopy:
reading the light a target reflects or gives off, and using the pattern in that light to identify
minerals that look similar to the eye.

Two current examples show how the technique is used. On NASA's Perseverance rover — a real
mission, unrelated to Red Horizon — SuperCam combines several spectroscopic methods, including
laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy and Raman, in a single instrument, and its mast unit was
built by a [French-led consortium](https://cnes.fr/en/projects/supercam). ESA's Rosalind
Franklin rover carries the
[infrared spectrometer Enfys](https://explorationscience.esa.int/project/exomars-rosalind-franklin-rfm/),
which works alongside its panorama cameras on mineral composition.

## Watching the weather

Weather measurement is what turns a set of rock measurements into a picture of a place. It
records what the atmosphere is doing now and how those conditions change over a season, which
matters twice: for the science, and for operations, because wind, dust, and temperature set the
limits on when a rover can work and how clear its images will be.

NASA's Perseverance carries a dedicated weather station, MEDA, which measures wind, temperature,
humidity, and airborne dust. Weather sensing of that kind is now a normal part of a surface
science payload rather than an add-on.

## Getting close enough to inspect

The fourth job is contact inspection: examining a rock surface or a patch of soil at a scale the
mast cameras cannot reach. Perseverance's SHERLOC instrument works with the WATSON camera at the
end of the rover's arm; Rosalind Franklin's CLUPI is a close-up imager designed for the same kind
of work. In both cases the goal is detail that a wide view loses — grain size, texture, and the
weathered rind of a rock.

## A European-led contribution

The Red Horizon coalition is ten partner groups with one vote each, whatever they contribute.
Instrumentation is one of the places where that structure shows, and a European-led sensor
contribution is part of the selected payload scope. Its exact name and the technique it will use
are still open, and the programme will not describe them as settled until they are.

## Every instrument needs a home

Selection is not only a science exercise. Each instrument needs a defined physical place on the
vehicle, and the team has to show that it can be mounted, pointed, and operated without
interfering with the others. An instrument that cannot be accommodated is not really selected,
however good the measurement would be. That is why a compact, explainable payload takes
precedence over a longer list of capabilities.

## What is not settled

- The individual instruments, and their names, have not been approved.
- A European-led sensor contribution is in scope, but its name and technique are still open.
- No launch vehicle has been named, and no landing site has been chosen publicly.
- The schedule frame remains the 2031 launch window set out in the [programme announcement](/#updates).
  No launch or landing date is being stated.

## What this mission is not

Red Horizon One is not a life-detection mission. It carries no experiment designed to prove that
life exists or existed on Mars. Its science goals are narrower and more answerable: mapping,
mineral context, environmental monitoring, and careful work on the material it examines at the
surface. The question is whether a site once offered conditions that could have supported life as
science understands it — not whether anything lived there.
