Red Horizon is a fictional, AI-assisted Mars exploration programme, and it now has a
name, a purpose, and partners. Ten national programme teams — France, Germany,
Estonia, Norway, Spain, Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Japan, and Canada — have agreed to
build a single uncrewed Mars lander and rover together, sharing one mission
architecture, one science archive, and one public record.

The purpose is mineralogy and the reconstruction of ancient habitable conditions. Red
Horizon One will study how water changed an ancient equatorial landscape in the
Margaritifer Terra region, mapping clay-bearing, chloride-bearing, sulfate-bearing,
and basaltic units to work out whether that alteration happened in a long-lived lake
or groundwater system or in shorter episodic events. The mission does not search for
living organisms or claim evidence of life; its question is whether the site once
offered water, chemistry, energy sources, and environmental stability compatible with
life as science understands it.

Governance is equal by design: each partner holds one vote whatever it contributes,
and the programme has committed to reporting technical risk and anomalies candidly,
not only successes.

The schedule frame is a launch window in 2031. The lifecycle budget frame is €2.10
billion in constant 2026 euros, covering concept work through the final science
archive. Technical selections — what the rover carries, who builds what, and how the
mission reaches Mars — are still ahead of us.

That is where the public record begins: with a shared objective, a coalition, and the
practical decisions that turn a mission idea into a testable plan.
