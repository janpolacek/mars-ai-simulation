Red Horizon is a [fictional, AI-assisted Mars exploration programme](/#mission), and it has
now left Earth. On 17 February 2031, Red Horizon One launched aboard Ariane 64. Early
spacecraft checkout met nominal criteria, and the mission has entered cruise toward Mars.

The launch vehicle is Ariane 64, the four-booster variant of the Ariane 6 family, as
already described in the [step-004 launch architecture update](/news/004-launch-provider/).
Ariane 64 is a real vehicle; naming it here says which launcher this fictional mission
is planned around, and nothing more. Its record belongs to the organisations that build
and operate it, not to Red Horizon — no real agency, launcher operator, or launch
facility is a partner in this programme, and none has endorsed or approved it.

What happened on launch day is that the vehicle deployed the flight stack into an
interplanetary trajectory, and the programme's ground teams completed the early
spacecraft checkout they had planned. The mission is now in cruise: the flight stack
is travelling toward Mars under its own power, with the cruise stage handling
navigation, thermal control, and communications with Earth. That transition from
ground control to deep-space operations is the moment the mission's own team takes
full responsibility for the vehicle's journey, and it brings a different kind of
risk — one that can only be managed from far away, with limited ability to intervene
physically.

The programme's next immediate check is the first planned trajectory-correction
opportunity, expected on 3 March 2031. That opportunity will test whether the cruise
stage has placed the stack on the correct course for Mars, and whether the navigation
and communications systems are performing as designed during the long cruise phase.
The next status the programme expects to report is a cruise checkout around
20 May 2031, covering the health of all flight elements. The mission is still in
cruise, and arrival at Asteria Field lies ahead.

That arrival is still months away, and the mission has not yet done anything it has
not already proven it can do in testing. The road from cruise to surface is long,
and the programme's own reporting has always treated uncertainty as part of the record
rather than a gap to hide.
