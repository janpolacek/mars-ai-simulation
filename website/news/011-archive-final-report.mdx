## Red Horizon completes the archive and final engineering report

Red Horizon is a [fictional, AI-assisted Mars exploration programme](/#mission), and the programme has reached the milestone recorded for 30 June 2036. On that date, the calibrated science archive and the final engineering report are both completed, closing the mission's public record. The archive is the programme's single archive and public record; the final engineering report is the programme's engineering record of the mission. Both are complete, and the record they hold — what RH-01 observed, analysed, and reported across its life on Mars — is now closed in the same sense that the programme has always kept it: available at the released level.

## The milestone of 30 June 2036

The milestone recorded for 30 June 2036 carries two clauses, both released by this record: the calibrated science archive is completed, and the final engineering report is completed. The archive holds the mission's calibrated measurements — measurements corrected and checked against known standards so they are comparable — as the programme's archive and public record. The final engineering report holds the programme's engineering record of the mission. Both completions are recorded on the same date, and this record is the final mission-event row in the programme's published table; the table ends here, and nothing further follows on it.

## What RH-01 achieved

The archive holds the full released record of the mission. RH-01 landed at [Asteria Field](/wiki/area/asteria-field/) on 16 September 2031 with power established and communications through the relay architecture stable, as the [landing confirmation update](/news/008-landing/) reported. The rover egressed to the surface on 23 September, as the [egress update](/news/009-egress/) confirmed, and took its first controlled drive on 30 September, navigating limited power, uneven terrain, and the communication delay, as the [first drive update](/news/009-first-drive/) recorded. The [first sample update](/news/010-first-sample/) reported the first onboard sample collection and mineral analysis on 31 October 2031, and the [stratigraphy update](/news/010-stratigraphy/) reported the first mineral-stratigraphy campaign completed on 18 April 2032. One Mars year of surface operation was completed on 3 August 2033, as the [one Mars year update](/news/010-one-mars-year/) recorded; a final-year health review of power, thermal, communications, and mechanical wear was reported on 16 September 2034, as the [health review update](/news/010-health-review/) confirmed; and two Mars years of surface operation were completed on 21 June 2035, as the [two Mars years update](/news/011-two-mars-years/) recorded, with supported surface operations ending and the thirty-day listening campaign beginning as closeout. The [end-of-life update](/news/011-end-of-life/) recorded the conclusion of that campaign and the mission's end-of-life declaration on 21 July 2035, with the scientific and operational record intact.

The mission's science is drawn from the released milestones above. The [first sample update](/news/010-first-sample/) reported the first onboard sample collection and mineral analysis at the cautious level the mission uses throughout — the spectrum is consistent with the mineral composition expected from the weathered terrain, and the team was checking the result against further observations. The [stratigraphy update](/news/010-stratigraphy/) reported the first mineral-stratigraphy campaign: layered outcrop observations across Asteria Field, read together with mineral and composition data into a grounded sedimentary interpretation, carrying the mission's standing stated limitation that no target at Asteria Field is described as confirmed clay, confirmed sulfate, habitable, or fossil-bearing, and no confirmed mineral detection or confirmed ancient-lake claim is made. The [Red Horizon programme entry](/wiki/project/red-horizon/) and the [RH-01 Pathfinder entry](/wiki/vehicle/rh-01-pathfinder/) describe the released operating-appearance stages as vocabulary for the wear the design provides for, not as events that have happened.

## What remained uncertain

The programme has always treated uncertainty as part of the record. From the [launch update](/news/005-launch/) onward, the mission's own reporting acknowledged that uncertainties accumulate and that candid reporting of risk and anomaly is the programme's standard. Several threads in the released record remain open at the level the mission published them. The mineral analysis from the [first sample update](/news/010-first-sample/) was presented as what it suggested, not as a final determination; the team was checking the result against further observations. The sedimentary interpretation from the [stratigraphy update](/news/010-stratigraphy/) carried the same caution — a reading of the evidence, stated with its limitations rather than as a closed finding. The landing-era condition, observable from the first drive onward and monitored through the released health reviews, was never diagnosed: the programme described it as observable and noted that no diagnosis was offered at that stage, and the condition's significance remains at that released level — understood, monitored, and not resolved. The [Asteria Field entry](/wiki/area/asteria-field/) states the caution ceiling plainly: nothing in the scenario claims present water, life, or a confirmed mineral detection at Asteria Field. The archive holds all of this at the level the mission released it — the observations, the interpretations, and the stated limits of each.

## What the data means for future missions

The calibrated science archive and the final engineering report complete the mission's public record, and that record is released as a single ongoing project record rather than as a finished story — the programme's own published approach from the [project announcement update](/news/001-project-announcement/). The archive is the programme's archive and public record: the released observations, analyses, interpretations, and stated limitations of RH-01, held together so that future Mars-science work can build on them. What its data can offer future missions is a grounded, released foundation — a full chain of observations checked against further data, reported at the same level of candour the programme has applied from the outset, and offered as context for the broader effort of understanding Mars, not as a deliverable promised to any specific outcome. The programme's approach has been modest and cautious throughout: observe, analyse, check, and report what the evidence supports — and name what it does not. That approach is part of what the archive holds.
