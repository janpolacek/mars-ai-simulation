## Identity and name

The launch vehicle documented in this dossier is the **Ariane 64**. It is the four-booster variant of the Ariane 6 launch-vehicle family. The vehicle designation is the public string the article, the wiki page, the dossier, and any approved asset use verbatim; it is the single token the programme names when it names its launcher.

The Ariane 64 designation is the **launch vehicle designation** that scenario canon releases. It is a fact about the vehicle, not an endorsement, contract, or implied participation by its real operator, industrial prime contractor, or launch facility. No real institution is named on any public surface as a result of this dossier existing.

The launch vehicle is documented in two parts and only in this dossier:

1. **The real provider's public record** — the launcher's documented public history, configuration, and operations context, cited from real public sources, separated from the programme's interface.
2. **The fictional programme's interface** — the payload adapter, the injection segment, the integration campaign as the mission uses them, and the target spacecraft mass at separation, written in programme-specific terms and never presented as the real provider's figures.

No sentence in this dossier implies participation, approval, funding, or endorsement by any real provider, operator, or institution. ## Role in the flight stack

The Ariane 64 is the first element of the Red Horizon One flight system. It places the cruise stage onto the Earth-departure trajectory that carries the mission toward Mars. The four other elements of the flight system (the cruise stage, the aeroshell and powered stationary landing platform, RH-01 Pathfinder, and the ground and relay segment) are downstream of the launcher and depend on it only through the launch-vehicle interface.

`docs/SCENARIO.md:122-127` lists the four flight elements that follow the launcher in the programme's architecture:

> 1. an Ariane 64 injection and payload-adapter segment;
> 2. a solar-powered cruise stage for navigation, thermal control, and Earth communications;
> 3. an aeroshell and powered stationary landing platform for entry, descent, landing, rover deployment, and surface relay;
> 4. RH-01 Pathfinder, a roughly two-metre, six-wheel rover matching the canonical vehicle dossier.

The Ariane 64's role in this stack is limited to the first few minutes of the mission: lift-off, ascent, fairing jettison, and injection onto the outbound trajectory. Once the cruise stage separates, the launcher has no further role. The cruise stage handles navigation, thermal control, and Earth communications; the landing platform handles entry, descent, landing, rover deployment, and surface relay; RH-01 Pathfinder handles the surface science campaign. The launcher's contribution is the energy to leave Earth's gravity well on the right trajectory; everything after separation belongs to a different element of the stack.

The target spacecraft mass at launch-vehicle separation is **4,250 kg**, including system margin. This is the programme-controlled mass ceiling — what the mission design protects, not what a real launcher is contracted to deliver. `docs/SCENARIO.md:255-258` is explicit: the €180 million launch-service line in the lifecycle budget is a programme planning allowance, not a price or commitment from any real supplier. Currency, year, scope, and accounting differences prevent direct price comparison with any real launch service, and the dossier does not attempt one.

## Visual identity and silhouette

### Proportions and silhouette

The Ariane 64 silhouette is defined by the four-booster configuration of the Ariane 6 family: a central core stage flanked by four solid rocket boosters, a Vulcain 2.1 main engine on the core, and a payload fairing on top. The vehicle is tall and slender, with a body diameter defined by the core stage and a launch mass class consistent with a heavy-lift launcher. The defining feature that distinguishes the Ariane 64 from the two-booster Ariane 62 is the pair of additional solid rocket boosters strapped to the core, symmetrically placed to give a balanced four-booster outline.

The Ariane 64 is a real launch vehicle. The silhouette section below documents the launcher's documented public geometry — its real configuration, its real payload-adapter interface, and its real launch-site framing — at the level the canon fixes. No editorial scene image is implied: no on-pad photo, no in-transit stack, no integration campaign picture. The precedent in `docs/vehicles/pathfinder/` is the studio / neutral-floor render style of the RH-01 plates, where the vehicle is shown against a clean white background, on a white floor, with broad soft light and a faint grounded shadow. A launch-vehicle plate in this style would show the Ariane 64 silhouette without context: no launch tower, no service gantry, no mobile gantry, no crew, no integration equipment, and no human figure. The launch site and its equipment live in a separate plate, generic and not specific to the launch complex that real operations use.

### Body and configuration

The central core is a single liquid-hydrogen / liquid-oxygen stage with the Vulcain 2.1 main engine. The four solid rocket boosters are mounted symmetrically on the core, providing the bulk of lift-off thrust. Above the core sits the upper stage with the Vinci engine, and above that the payload fairing and the payload adapter that carries the spacecraft stack. The configuration is single-stick: one core, four strap-on boosters, no additional core, no cross-feed.

The body is not crew-rated. It has no crew access arm, no launch-escape system, no crew capsule, and no life-support hardware. It is an uncrewed heavy-lift launcher for automated payloads; its environmental control is for the payload, not for people. The interior of the fairing is conditioned for the spacecraft during the ascent phase only.

The vehicle is not reusable. The core stage, the upper stage, and the solid rocket boosters are all expended; none is recovered, none is refurbished, and none flies again. No catch mechanism, no landing legs, no recovery boats, and no retrieval infrastructure are part of the Ariane 64 silhouette. The launch campaign treats each flight as a single-use event.

### Four-booster arrangement

The four solid rocket boosters are the defining engineering feature of the Ariane 64. They are arranged two on each side of the core, top-and-bottom on each side, giving the vehicle its square cross-section at the booster attach points and its rectangular outline at lift-off. The booster arrangement is fixed: there are no extra boosters hidden in the fairing, no drop-off test articles, no additional strap-ons beyond the four, and no asymmetric configurations. The "64" in the designation refers to the four-booster variant of the Ariane 6 family.

The boosters are visible from below as the most prominent external feature of the lift-off silhouette. They are smooth white cylinders, similar in diameter to the core, with a clean separation plane where they meet the core structure. Each booster has a single nozzle at its base, and the nozzles are aligned with the core nozzle. There is no second engine cluster, no auxiliary power unit on the booster exterior, and no aerodynamic strake.

### Payload adapter and fairing interface

The Ariane 64 carries the spacecraft stack under a payload fairing on top of the upper stage. The fairing is jettisoned once the ascent environment no longer requires it, typically shortly after passing through the densest part of the atmosphere. The payload adapter is the mechanical interface between the launcher and the spacecraft; it is the single attachment point the cruise stage sees at separation.

The spacecraft stack for Red Horizon One is a single payload under the fairing: the cruise stage with the aeroshell and landing platform mounted on it, and RH-01 Pathfinder stowed inside the aeroshell. The stack is one continuous mechanical chain from the payload adapter up through the cruise stage, the aeroshell, and into the rover. The fairing protects the stack during ascent; the adapter transmits launch loads to the cruise stage; the cruise stage carries the rest.

### Materials and surface character

The Ariane 64 baseline studio appearance is white: white core, white boosters, white fairing, with the dark Vulcain 2.1 nozzle and the dark upper-stage Vinci nozzle as the only large dark features on the body. The white is not a glossy showroom white; it has fine surface variation, the kind of soft tonal separation that real flight hardware carries, and enough contrast with the sky or a neutral background to keep the body readable. There are no decorative graphics, no painted mascots, no national flags, no large logos, and no bold accent colours. Markings are small and technical: panel boundaries, access doors, separation plane lines, and small technical labels in restrained positions.

The baseline studio appearance places the full-size launcher on a seamless neutral white floor with a white background, broad soft light, and a faint grounded shadow. The body is slightly darker than the background so the white panels retain their edges. The result is an engineering reference appearance, with no launch tower, no service gantry, no mobile gantry, no crew, no smoke, no plume, no flame, no integration equipment, no launch-site infrastructure, and no graphic overlay. A second plate may show the launcher against a generic launch-site setting — neutral concrete, a generic service structure outline, no launch complex markings — if the visuals role decides the moment is shown that way; no real launch facility is depicted.

### Design character

The Ariane 64 is distinct through its single-core four-booster configuration, white body, central payload adapter, and short fairing. The overall character is tall, slender, heavy-lift, and uncrewed. There is no crew-rated hardware, no reusable hardware, no on-pad integration equipment, and no launch-complex-specific geometry.

## Interface to the programme's payload

The programme's interface to the Ariane 64 is the **payload-adapter and injection-segment** framing at `docs/SCENARIO.md:124`. The interface is what the spacecraft stack sees at the moment of separation, and it is the only point where the launcher and the mission touch.

The four elements of the programme's interface are:

1. **Payload adapter.** A standard mechanical interface between the Ariane 64 upper stage and the cruise stage. The adapter transmits launch loads and separates cleanly on command once the upper stage reaches the right point on the outbound trajectory. The cruise stage treats the adapter as a single attachment point and does not see the rest of the launcher.
2. **Injection segment.** The portion of the ascent that places the spacecraft stack on the Earth-departure trajectory. The upper stage performs the final injection burn after fairing jettison; the cruise stage separates once the trajectory is correct. The injection segment ends at separation, and the launcher has no further role.
3. **Target mass.** 4,250 kg at launch-vehicle separation, including system margin. This is a programme-controlled mass ceiling (`docs/SCENARIO.md:129`), not a launcher performance figure. It is what the mission design protects, not what the launcher is contracted to deliver.
4. **Integration campaign.** The programme-side process that delivers the spacecraft stack to the launch site, integrates it with the launcher, and supports it through launch. The campaign is a fictional programme activity: it does not name a real launch facility, a real operator, or a real integration team. The real integration infrastructure (the launch complex, the mobile gantry, the payload preparation facilities) is documented separately under "Real provider's public record" below.

The 17 February 2031 launch and the 210.7-day outbound transfer are scenario planning anchors (`docs/SCENARIO.md:175-177`, `:193`). They are private scenario canon. Step 004 carries only the **2031 launch window** as released by article 001; the launch day, time, and date are owned by timeline step `005-launch` and must not appear in article 004 prose, alt text, or captions.

The launch window itself — "2031" — is the only calendar information the article 004 prose may carry from this dossier.

## What the vehicle must never be said to be

The following list is binding on every card that touches public text. It is copied verbatim into the article 004 brief and the containment checklist, and it binds prose, alt text, captions, asset names, slugs, SEO packages, and commit messages.

The Ariane 64 must **never** be described as any of the following:

- **Crew-rated.** It has no crew access arm, no launch-escape system, no crew capsule, and no life-support hardware. It is an uncrewed launcher for automated payloads.
- **Reusable.** The core stage, the upper stage, and the solid rocket boosters are expended. None is recovered, none is refurbished, none flies again.
- **In transit.** It is a launcher, not a spacecraft; once it has placed the cruise stage on the outbound trajectory, it does not continue in flight.
- **Flight-ready.** The 17 February 2031 launch is private calendar canon, owned by timeline step `005-launch`. Step 004 is a design decision, not a launch readiness statement.
- **On the pad.** No on-pad image, no launch-tower image, no service-gantry image, no mobile-gantry image, no launch-site image specific to a real launch facility is part of step 004 public copy.
- **Integrated at the campaign site.** The integration campaign is a programme activity described in programme terms; the real launch facility, the real launch complex, and the real integration team are not named.
- **Endorsed, funded, partnered with, or approved by any real institution.** Any sentence that implies participation, approval, funding, or endorsement by a real provider, operator, or institution violates the standing rule at `docs/SCENARIO.md:281`.
- **Carrying a contracted payload mass to Mars.** The 4,250 kg target is the programme-controlled mass ceiling, not a launcher performance figure. Any sentence that presents the figure as the launcher's contracted performance misrepresents both the figure and the launcher.

Any downstream card that wants to add a sentence covering one of these negations must consult the human story owner through Gate 004-B (or the appropriate gate for the moment) before publishing.
